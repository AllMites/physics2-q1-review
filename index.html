TOPIC 01 · ACTIVITY 1.7

Wave
Diffraction

Waves refuse to travel in straight lines. They lean around corners.

Target: what you must be able to do

  • demonstrate wave diffraction
  • define and characterize wave diffraction
  • analyze diagrams for diffracted waves

Hook · The breakwater

A breakwater is a wall built offshore to protect boats and stop the beach eroding. It has a gap in it.

Straight, nearly plane waves roll in. They squeeze through the gap. And then, they don't just continue straight through as a neat beam.

They fan out into semi-circles and reach water that is directly behind the solid wall. Water that, by pure geometry, should be perfectly calm.

Notice three things

  1. Waves get into the shadow region, the area geometry says is blocked.
  2. Wavefront shape changes: plane → semi-circular.
  3. Energy spreads sideways, perpendicular to the original direction of travel.

Ignite · The definition

Diffraction is the bending of waves around barriers, obstacles, and through openings.

Waves coming from the upper left hit an obstacle, bend around it, and keep propagating into the shadow region behind it.

The condition: when is diffraction obvious?

Diffraction is most apparent when the obstacle or gap is about the same size as the wavelength.

Same order of magnitude. A 2 cm ripple meeting a 2 cm gap fans out beautifully. A 2 cm ripple meeting a 5 m gap barely notices it happened.

Knot · Answer these

1What happens to incident waves at the obstacle? Compare different sizes and shapes.

The waves bend around the edges of the obstacle and propagate into the shadow region behind it, instead of leaving a sharply-cut calm patch.

Comparing: obstacles whose size is close to the wavelength produce obvious bending, the shadow fills in quickly and almost disappears. Obstacles much larger than the wavelength cast a clear, long shadow with only slight curling at the edges. Shape controls the pattern's geometry (a curved obstacle spreads waves differently from a flat-faced block), but bending happens at every edge regardless of shape.

Waves bend around edges into the shadow. Small obstacle (≈ λ) → strong bending. Large obstacle (≫ λ) → clear shadow, slight edge curl.
2Compare the directions of the incident and diffracted waves.

The incident waves travel in one single direction, their rays are parallel and their wavefronts are straight (plane waves).

The diffracted waves travel in many directions, fanning out over a range of angles from the gap or edge. Their rays radiate outward and their wavefronts are curved. The original direction is still present in the middle of the fan, it's the strongest one, but energy now also travels at angles to it.

Incident = one direction, straight fronts. Diffracted = many directions, curved fronts spreading sideways.
3Does λ change after diffracting? What about f, T and v? Explain.

None of them change. This is the most-missed question in the whole activity.

  • Frequency f is fixed by the source, the ruler or the generator. A barrier is a passive object; it can't change how fast the source vibrates.
  • Speed v is fixed by the medium, the water and its depth. Same water on both sides of the barrier, same speed.
  • Since λ = v/f and neither v nor f changed, λ is unchanged.
  • T = 1/f, so T is unchanged too.
λ, f, T, v all unchanged. Only direction and wavefront shape change.
4What happens after passing through different slit widths? Compare the 3 widths.

All three spread, but by different amounts. With the wavelength held constant:

  • Narrowest slit → greatest spreading. The gap behaves almost like a single point source, emitting near-semicircular wavefronts across a wide angle.
  • Medium slit → moderate spread. A recognisable straight-through beam with curved edges.
  • Widest slit → least spreading. The wave passes through as a beam roughly as wide as the gap, with only the edges curling.

So: narrower slit, more diffraction, because narrowing the slit makes the gap more comparable to the wavelength.

Narrower slit → more spreading. Wider slit → straighter beam, less diffraction.
5Relate wavelength to the degree of diffraction (procedure 5).

With slit width held constant and wavelength varied via frequency:

Longer wavelength (lower frequency) → more diffraction.
Shorter wavelength (higher frequency) → less diffraction.

The degree of diffraction depends on the ratio λ/w, not on λ alone. Diffraction becomes strong once λ is comparable to the slit width, which is the same statement as "diffraction is most apparent when the obstacle size is the same order of magnitude as the wavelength."

Everyday proof: you can hear someone talking around a corner but you can't see them. Sound has wavelengths of about a metre, comparable to a doorway, so it diffracts hugely. Light has wavelengths of about half a micrometre, a doorway is a million times bigger, so light goes straight.

Degree of diffraction ∝ λ/w. Bigger λ relative to the gap → more bending.

Knot · carry this out of the room

Big wave, small gap → big bend.
Small wave, big gap → straight through.

TOPIC 02 · ACTIVITY 1.8

Wave
Interference

Two waves meet. They do not bounce. They do not break. They add, and then walk away unharmed.

Target

  • demonstrate wave interference
  • define interference and differentiate the types of wave interference
  • analyze and draw diagrams for interference of waves

Hook · Why is the sea so messy?

A photo of real water shows a complicated criss-crossing pattern, nothing like the neat circles or straight lines you made in a tray. That complexity isn't a new kind of wave. It is just simple waves from different sources, superposed. That phenomenon is interference.

Ignite · The one rule

When two waves pass the same point in space at the same time, the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.

yresult = y₁ + y₂   Algebraic, signs included. Up is positive, down is negative. That minus sign is the whole subject.
Two pulses meet: watch what survivestop: crest + trough  ·  bottom: crest + crest
top row is nowCOMPLETE DESTRUCTIVE
bottom row is nowCONSTRUCTIVE

Drag pulse 2's amplitude below 1.00 and the top row stops cancelling completely, that is partial destructive interference. Equal-and-opposite is the only way to get a perfectly flat string.

The four cases: know these cold

TypeConditionResult
Constructive in phasecrest meets crest, or trough meets troughresultant is greater than either wave. Two equal pulses give twice the amplitude.
Complete destructive out of phasecrest meets trough, amplitudes equalthey cancel exactly, momentarily zero, a flat string.
Partial destructive out of phasecrest meets trough, amplitudes unequala wave of reduced amplitude. Something is left over.
Partial interference neitherneither in phase nor out of phaseneither constructive nor destructive, an in-between resultant.

Phase: the word for "line them up"

Phase describes the relative positions of the crests of two waves.

  • In phase, crests aligned with crests, troughs with troughs → constructive.
  • Out of phase, crests of one aligned with troughs of the other → destructive (complete or partial, depending on the amplitudes).
  • Neither → partial interference.

The point everybody forgets

Interference only happens while the waves overlap.

After they pass through each other, both pulses resume their original shapes and carry on in the same directions as before. A wave is not damaged by another wave. Cancellation is temporary, not destruction.

Two-point source patterns

Two point sources of the same frequency each pump out concentric circular wavefronts. In the standard diagram, thick lines are crests and thin lines are troughs, frozen at one instant of time.

● Red dots: ANTINODES

Where crest meets crest (thick + thick) or trough meets trough (thin + thin). Points of constructive interference. The water there heaves up and down the most. Join them up and you get an antinodal line.

● Blue dots: NODES

Where a crest meets a trough (thick + thin). Points of destructive interference. The water there stays still. Join them up and you get a nodal line.

Knot · Answer these

1What happens as the two waves meet and overlap? What is the result?

They superpose, they pass through the same points at the same time and their displacements add algebraically.

The result is a new pattern of larger and smaller disturbance: an interference pattern. Where crests coincide with crests the water rises more than either wave alone; where crests coincide with troughs the water is momentarily undisturbed. Neither wave is destroyed, what you're seeing is their sum.

They superpose; resultant displacement = algebraic sum → an interference pattern of nodes and antinodes.
2Can you identify the constructive and destructive areas? How?

Constructive (antinodes): the areas of largest disturbance, brightest/darkest bands in the shadow, water visibly heaving. Found where crest meets crest or trough meets trough.

Destructive (nodes): the areas that look flat and still, uniform grey in the shadow, no visible up-down motion. Found where crest meets trough.

Both appear as lines radiating outward from the region between the two sources (nodal lines and antinodal lines), not as isolated dots.

Antinodes = maximum motion (crest+crest / trough+trough). Nodes = no motion (crest+trough). Both form radiating lines.
3What happens to the two waves after interfering?

They carry on completely unchanged, each keeps its original shape, amplitude, wavelength, frequency and direction of travel, as though the other wave had never been there.

Interference exists only during the overlap. It is a temporary sum, not a permanent collision. This is why two people can talk across each other in a room and both voices still arrive intact.

They pass through each other and resume their original shapes and directions. Interference is temporary.
4What happens to the pattern when you change the distance between the sources?

With wavelength held constant:

  • Small separation → few nodal and antinodal lines, widely spread. At very small d, almost the whole field is one big antinodal region.
  • Medium separation → more lines appear.
  • Large separation → many lines, closely packed together.

Rule: increasing the separation increases the number of nodal and antinodal lines, because more whole wavelengths fit into the path difference between the two sources.

Greater separation → more nodal and antinodal lines, more tightly packed.
5What happens when you change the wavelength of both sources?

With separation held constant:

  • Long wavelength (low frequency) → fewer lines, widely spread apart.
  • Short wavelength (high frequency) → more lines, packed closer.

Same underlying reason as Q4: the pattern depends on how many wavelengths fit into the path difference. Shrinking λ fits more in, so more lines. Combined statement: the number of lines is governed by the ratio d/λ.

Shorter λ → more lines. Longer λ → fewer lines. Everything is controlled by d/λ.

Ignite · The principle, stated formally

Principle of Linear Superposition

When two or more waves are present simultaneously at the same place, the resultant amplitude is the sum of the amplitudes of the individual waves.

Cutnell & Johnson (2012), p. 522

You only need one extra tool

d = v t d = distance each pulse has travelled from where it started (cm) · v = wave speed (cm/s) · t = elapsed time (s)

The whole method, in two moves:
1. Slide each pulse along by d = vt, independently, as if the other one didn't exist.
2. Add the two heights together point by point to draw the resultant.

Knot · the takeaway

Overlap is a moment, not an event.

At t = 2 s the string looks like one tall pulse. At t = 4 s it looks exactly like t = 0 s, just further apart. Nothing was consumed, nothing was created. Superposition is arithmetic, not collision.

TOPIC 04 · LESSON 2.1

Introduction
to SHM

Out of every possible back-and-forth motion in the universe, exactly one kind is simple. This is the entry exam for it.

Target

  • define and identify the condition for simple harmonic motion
  • recognize displacement, amplitude, frequency, and period in SHM

Hook · Periodic motion is everywhere

Periodic motion = back-and-forth motion over the same path, with each oscillation taking the same amount of time. The three examples the lesson gives:

⏱ Grandfather clock

The swinging bob. Same arc, same time, for a century.

⌚ Quartz crystal

A vibrating crystal in a watch. 32,768 oscillations every second.

🚗 Engine pistons

Up and down the same cylinder, thousands of times a minute.

Periodic motion is so common that it's worth building proper machinery for, and it's the foundation for waves: sound and light are both this idea, repeated in space.

Ignite · The simplest possible oscillator

A mass m on a massless spring, free to slide on a frictionless horizontal track.

Equilibrium position

Where the mass sits when the spring is neither compressed nor stretched. The spring is at its natural length and exerts no force on m. We call this x = 0.

Restoring force

Pull the mass right → spring pulls it left. Push it left → spring pushes it right. Either way the spring tries to restore the mass to equilibrium. The restoring force always opposes the displacement.

F = −k x F = restoring force (N) · k = spring constant, the stiffness (N/m) · x = displacement from equilibrium (m)
The minus sign is not decoration. It IS the physics: force and displacement point opposite ways.

The condition for SHM: the single most important line in this topic

Motion is simple harmonic when the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement and opposite in direction.

F ∝ −x. Linear in x, and negative. Break either half and it isn't SHM.

When this holds, position varies sinusoidally with time.

Knot · the summary

  • A restoring force acting continuously makes a body oscillate about an equilibrium point.
  • Displacement x is position relative to equilibrium.
  • When the restoring force is opposite and directly proportional to displacement, it's SHM, and position varies sinusoidally with time.
  • Amplitude A = maximum displacement. One cycle = back and forth to the same point.
  • Period T = seconds per cycle. Frequency f = cycles per second (Hz). They're reciprocals.
TOPIC 05 · LESSON 2.2

Energy
& SHM

Why doesn't the mass just stop in the middle where nothing is pulling it?

Target

  • explain energy transformations in SHM
  • explain how force, velocity and acceleration change as an object undergoes SHM

Hook · The question that runs this whole topic

At the equilibrium position the spring exerts zero force. So why does the mass shoot straight past it instead of stopping?

Hold that thought. By the end of this section you'll answer it in four words.

Ignite · The setup and why it's clean

  • Surface is frictionless → the spring force F = −kx is the only horizontal force.
  • Weight and normal force are vertical while the motion is horizontal → they do no work.
  • The spring force is a conservative force → total mechanical energy is conserved.
ME = K + U = ½mv² + ½kx² = constant K = kinetic energy of the mass (J) · U = elastic potential energy of the spring-mass system (J) · ME = total mechanical energy (J)

K and U trade back and forth constantly. Their sum never moves.

The three places that matter

CASE A At the extremes, x = ±A

  • Restoring force is at its maximum: F_max = −kA.
  • By Newton's 2nd law, acceleration is therefore also maximum here.
  • But the mass momentarily stops before changing direction → v = 0, so K = 0.
ME = ½m(0)² + ½kA² = ½kA² All the energy is elastic potential energy.

Huge consequence: ME ∝ A². The total energy depends on the square of the amplitude. Double the amplitude and you quadruple the energy.

CASE B At equilibrium, x = 0

  • Restoring force is zero → acceleration is zero.
  • Spring is neither stretched nor compressed → U = 0.
  • All the energy has become kinetic → the mass is moving at maximum velocity.
ME = ½mv²max + ½k(0)² = ½mv²max

So, the Hook answer, in four words: it still has velocity. Zero force doesn't mean zero speed. Force controls change in velocity, not velocity itself. The mass sails through, the spring starts opposing it, it slows, stops, and comes back.

CASE C Anywhere else

Velocity and acceleration are both non-zero, and the energy is a mixture of kinetic and potential. Nothing special happens here, which is itself the point.

Bridging the two cases

Sinusoidal
Nature of SHM

Everything so far was words. Here is where it becomes numbers.

Target

solve problems, both graphically and by calculation, on acceleration, velocity, and position during SHM

Hook · The bungee jumper

A jumper on a bungee cord oscillates up and down. Track them:

From equilibrium going up, they slow down until they stop at the highest point, then fall. They speed up back to equilibrium, where they're moving fastest. Past it, they keep going down but slowing until they momentarily stop at the lowest point. Then back up again.

Velocity is maximum at equilibrium and zero at the highest and lowest points. Position and velocity both vary sinusoidally with time, that is the defining signature of SHM.

Ignite · The three equations

These are the forms for a body that is at the equilibrium position when t = 0.

x = A sin ωt Position. A = amplitude, maximum displacement (m). ω = angular frequency, a measure of how fast oscillations occur (rad/s). v = Aω cos ωt Velocity, the time derivative of position. Peak value: v_max = Aω a = −Aω² sin ωt Acceleration, the time derivative of velocity. Peak value: a_max = Aω²

Why v is a cosine

A cosine is a sine shifted by a quarter cycle. So velocity leads position by 90°. In plain words: v peaks exactly where x crosses zero, and v is zero exactly where x is at ±A.

Why a is also a sine: but negative

Acceleration has the same shape as position but the opposite sign, they are 180° out of phase. Max displacement and max acceleration happen at the same moment, pointing opposite ways. At x = 0 both force and acceleration are zero.

Vertical SHM · why gravity disappears

Hang the spring vertically and now two forces act: the spring force and the weight. Does that wreck the SHM?

(a) No mass

Spring hangs at its natural length.

(b) Mass attached

The spring stretches by s and the system settles at a new equilibrium:

k s = m g

(c) Displaced by y

Now push or pull it a further distance y from that new equilibrium.

Pick one axis and the whole thing takes three lines

Measure y downward from the new equilibrium, and count forces downward-positive. The spring is stretched by s + y, so it pulls up with k(s + y), while the weight mg pulls down:

Step 1, write the net forceF_net = mg − k(s + y)
Step 2, expand itF_net = mg − ks − ky
Step 3, apply the equilibrium condition ks = mgThose two terms cancel, leaving
F_net = −ky

One equation covers both sides. Push the mass down and y is positive, so the force comes out negative, pointing up toward equilibrium. Lift it up and y is negative, so the force comes out positive, pointing down toward equilibrium. The minus sign does the work in both directions.

↯ The lesson splits this into two cases and reports F_net = +ky for the mass below equilibrium. That plus sign is a bookkeeping artefact: the two cases measure y in opposite directions. A real +ky would be a runaway force, the exact thing ruled out back in Topic 4. Keep one axis and you get −ky every time.

In both cases the net force is pure Hooke's law, proportional to and opposite the displacement y.

Even though weight acts on the system, we can treat vertical SHM exactly like horizontal SHM, as long as we measure y from the NEW equilibrium position. Gravity's only job was to move the zero point. That's the whole trick.

Getting ω, f and T from k and m

Newton's second law with the SHM force: F_net = −ky = −kA sin ωt  and  F_net = ma = −mω²A sin ωt. Set them equal and everything except the constants cancels:

k = mω²  →  ω = √(k/m) f = (1/2π) √(k/m)     T = 2π √(m/k)

Sanity check them in words: k sits on top of the fraction inside ω: stiffer spring, faster wobble. m sits on the bottom: heavier mass, slower wobble. And A appears nowhere: the period of a spring does not depend on how far you pull it.

Ignite · What "simple" is doing in the name

A simple pendulum is an idealisation

  • the string is treated as massless
  • the bob is treated as a point particle
  • air resistance is negligible

Energy around the swing

Release the bob from rest at A (highest, right). It swings down through D (lowest, equilibrium) and up to G (highest, left), stops, and comes back.

Kinetic energy

Zero at A (released from rest). Increases as the bob approaches D. Maximum at D. Decreases as it moves away from D toward G. Zero at G.

Gravitational potential energy

Changes because the bob's height changes. Maximum at A and G (highest points). Decreases as it swings down to D. Minimum at D.

Potential energy decreases exactly as kinetic energy increases, and vice versa. Same trade as the spring, only now the storage tank is height instead of a stretched spring.

Free-body diagram · finding the restoring force

  • Two forces act on the bob: tension along the string, and the weight straight down.
  • The component of weight along the string, mg cos θ, is balanced by the tension, so nothing is left over along the string.
  • What's left is the component tangent to the path: mg sin θ, pointing toward the equilibrium position. On either side of the swing it still points home.

Strictly, tension slightly exceeds mg cos θ whenever the bob is moving, because the bob travels on an arc and needs a net inward force: T − mg cos θ = mv²/L. They are exactly equal only at the turning points, where v = 0. None of this touches the derivation below, which only uses the component along the path.

F = −m g sin θ The exact restoring force. The minus sign says "opposite the displacement", as always.
↯ sin θ is not proportional to θ. So strictly, a pendulum is NOT simple harmonic motion. We need one lie to make it work.

C1The pendulum on a cuckoo clock is 5.00 cm long. What is its frequency?
Step 1, convert unitsL = 5.00 cm = 0.0500 m
Step 2, frequency is the reciprocal of periodf = 1/T = (1/2π)√(g/L)  note g/L, flipped from the period formula
Step 3, substitutef = (1/2π)√(9.8/0.0500) = (1/2π)√196 = 14/(2π)
f ≈ 2.23 Hz  (T ≈ 0.449 s)

Sanity check: a tiny 5 cm pendulum should tick fast, twice a second sounds exactly like a cuckoo clock.

C2Trapeze performers swing on ropes and can be treated as a simple pendulum of length 16 m. Find the period for one complete back-and-forth cycle.
Step 1, straight substitutionT = 2π√(L/g) = 2π√(16/9.8)
Step 2, work the inside first16/9.8 = 1.6327,  √1.6327 = 1.2778
Step 3, multiplyT = 2π(1.2778) = 8.03 s
T ≈ 8.03 s

Note the performers' masses never enter the calculation, two trapeze artists of very different builds swing in perfect sync on equal ropes.

Knot · the summary

  • A pendulum has kinetic and gravitational potential energy, trading back and forth.
  • Maximum velocity at the equilibrium position (the lowest point). Zero velocity at the highest points.
  • For small angles (under 20°), the swinging of a simple pendulum is simple harmonic motion.
  • The period is directly proportional to the square root of the string's length: T = 2π√(L/g)

1A wave passes through a gap and spreads out. Which quantity changed?
Direction onlyFrequency is set by the source and speed is set by the medium, a barrier controls neither. Since λ = v/f and both are unchanged, λ is unchanged too. Diffraction changes where the wave goes, never what it is.
2You want more diffraction through a slit. What do you do?
Increase the wavelengthDiffraction is governed by the ratio λ/w. Raising λ (or narrowing w) raises the ratio and spreads the wave more. Raising the frequency would shorten λ and give you less diffraction, that's the trap. Amplitude changes how big the wave is, not how much it bends.
3Two pulses of unequal amplitude meet crest-to-trough. The result is:
Partial destructiveOut of phase, so they subtract, but unequal amplitudes can't cancel to zero. Something is left over: a wave of reduced amplitude. Complete destructive needs crest-to-trough and equal amplitudes. And waves never bounce off each other, they pass through.
4At a node in a two-source interference pattern:
Crest meets trough, destructiveNodes are points of destructive interference (the blue dots). Both waves do arrive there; they just cancel. Crest-meets-crest is an antinode, marked with red dots.
5Two rectangular pulses (each 1 unit tall, both pointing up, speed 1 cm/s) fully overlap. What is on the string, and what happens after?
Double the height, same width, then they separate unharmedSuperposition adds point by point, so heights add but widths don't. And overlap is temporary: each pulse resumes its original shape and original direction afterwards. Nothing is lost and nothing stays merged.
6Which force produces simple harmonic motion?
F = −7xSHM needs both halves of the condition: linear in x and negative. +6x is linear but pushes away from equilibrium, a runaway, not an oscillation. −3x² is negative but quadratic, and x² doesn't change sign, so it can't restore from both sides. −5 is a constant force, like gravity, no dependence on position at all.
7A mass in SHM is at x = +A. Which set is correct?
v = 0, a = max, K = 0, U = maxAt the extreme the mass momentarily stops (v = 0, so K = 0), while displacement is largest, so force and acceleration are largest, and all the energy sits in the spring as potential energy. Zero velocity does not mean zero acceleration. That option (the third one) is the classic trap.
8You double the amplitude of a spring oscillator. The total energy:
Four timesME = ½kA², energy goes as the square of amplitude, so (2)² = 4. Note the contrast: vmax and amax only double, because both are directly proportional to A. Energy is the odd one out, and that is precisely why it gets examined.
9At what position are the kinetic and potential energies equal?
x = A/√2, about 71% of the amplitudeK = U means each is half the total: ½kx² = ½(½kA²) → x² = A²/2 → x = ±A/√2. Because energy depends on x², the energy-halfway point is not the distance-halfway point. At x = A/2 the potential energy is only a quarter of the total.
10On a velocity–time graph of SHM, the points where the curve crosses zero are the moments when the mass is:
At maximum displacement, maximum forcev = 0 happens at the turning points, x = ±A. That's also where the v-curve is steepest, and slope of velocity is acceleration, so acceleration and force are maximum there. The last option is the trap: at rest, yes, but the force is at its largest, not zero. That force is what turns the mass around.
11A pendulum clock is taken to a mountain, where g is slightly smaller. The clock will:
Run slowT = 2π√(L/g), g is in the denominator, so smaller g gives a longer period. Longer per swing means fewer ticks per hour, so the clock falls behind. The quantity that cancels is mass, not g, g is the one thing besides length that the period depends on.
12To double the period of a pendulum, you must:
Four times the lengthT ∝ √L. To double T you need √L to double, which needs L to go up by 2² = 4. Mass doesn't appear in the formula at all, and release angle has no effect for small swings (isochronism).

Mistakes that cost the most marks

1 · Calculator in DEG

Every sin ωt and cos ωt needs RAD. Check it before you start, not after.

2 · "Zero velocity = zero force"

At x = ±A the mass is at rest and the force is at its maximum. That force is what turns it around.

3 · Half a cycle vs a full one

"Goes to the opposite side, crossing the middle once" = ½T. "Tick out and tock back" = full T.

4 · Energy doubling

Double A → 4× energy, not 2×. Only vmax and amax double.

5 · Wrong stretch as amplitude

A static hanging stretch used to find k is not the amplitude. Amplitude comes from where it was released from rest.

6 · Higher frequency = more diffraction

Backwards. Higher f means shorter λ, which means less bending.
